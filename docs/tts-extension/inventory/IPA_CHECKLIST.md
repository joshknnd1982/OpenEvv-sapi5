# IPA checklist

Written by `build_ipa_checklist.py` from `research/*.json`. Do not edit by hand; `IPA_CHECKLIST.json` is the same thing for programs.

**Chart:** The International Phonetic Alphabet, the issue marked "© 2026 IPA", "IPA Chart revised to 2015/2005" (<https://www.internationalphoneticassociation.org/content/ipa-chart>), CC BY-SA 4.0. The chart is cited, not reproduced. The IPA says the 2018 and 2020 issues were this same revision, and that it has re-issued the chart every year since 2025. Every section was also checked symbol by symbol against the human's copy, `IPA_Kiel.pdf`.

**175 entries**: one for every representation the chart prints. 12 of them are a second printed form of the same thing (each level and contour tone is printed as a diacritic and as a tone letter: 10; the ring above for voiceless beside the ring below: 1; the tie bar above beside the tie bar below: 1), which leaves **163 distinct things**: 107 letters and 56 marks.

Against the rough figure of 155: Wikipedia's article on the IPA (read 2026-10-05) counts 107 letters, 31 diacritics and 17 suprasegmental signs. The 107 letters are the same 107 here (59 pulmonic, 10 clicks and implosives, 10 other symbols, 28 vowels) and the 31 diacritics are the 31 rows of the diacritics table. The difference is in the rest: this checklist also has the ejective mark and the tie bar, which the chart prints outside the diacritics table, and counts 9 suprasegmentals and 14 tones and word accents (23) where that article counts 17; which of them it leaves out it does not say. Then 12 more for the second printed forms. 107 + 31 + 1 + 1 + 23 = 163 distinct; 163 + 12 = 175 entries.

## What the engine does today

Read from code and from the maps of the 145 packs. **Nothing was rendered or measured**: this says what the engine attempts, not whether it is right.

- **already**: letters: at least one of seven IBM modules (enus, engb, dede, eses, esus, frfr, itit: the ones whose phones engine/espeak_phonemes.py gives an IPA value for) has the sound as a phone of its own; frca, jajp and plpl were not consulted, so a sound only they have is under-reported. marks: the modules do it themselves.
- **partly**: letters: no module has it; the shipped packs say it as another module phone, reshaped by the accent layer, made by the accent layer, or as it is (a plain substitution). marks: a mechanism covers some cases, or some sounds so marked are defined one by one.
- **absent**: nothing in the shipped product says it.
- **unknown**: not determined in Phase 0.

### Expected acoustic correlates: consonants (non-pulmonic)

`literature` = found in a source the helper opened (named in brackets; `IPA_REFERENCES.md`). `recalled-unverified` = stated from memory with no opened source: to be verified before any value is used (it counts as `estimated`, playbook 1.5).

- **ʘ** (bilabial click (chart label: Bilabial); the letter gives the front (lip) closure only; the rear closure and its accompaniment (voiceless, voiced, nasal, aspirated...) need another letter):
  - a transient: a short, sharp release burst produced when the rarefied pocket of air between the lips and the tongue-back closure is opened, with no pulmonic aspiration unless the accompaniment adds it (`literature`) [wiki_click]
  - grouped with the 'noisy' (affricated, longer, friction-like) clicks rather than the 'abrupt' ones; a lip-released burst would be expected to have low-frequency (grave) energy (`literature`) [wiki_click]
  - click bursts as a class are louder than pulmonic stop bursts: in Traill's intensity scale for !Xóõ (reproduced in Miller & Shah) the click bursts rank above pulmonic t, k, q; Ladefoged & Traill (cited there) report click peaks often more than twice the peak-to-peak amplitude of the following vowel onset (about 6 dB) (`literature`) [miller_shah2009]
  - low-frequency emphasis of the burst (grave) is expected from the lip release; no fetched source gave a measured spectral value for this click (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ǀ** (dental click (chart label: Dental); laminal tongue-tip/blade front closure at the teeth, released with friction; rear closure set by the accompaniment):
  - noisy (fricated) release: a longer burst and a longer rise time to peak amplitude than the alveolar and palatal clicks (`literature`) [miller_shah2009, wiki_click]
  - the weakest burst of the four coronal clicks relative to the following vowel in Mangetti Dune !Xung (`literature`) [miller_shah2009]
  - highest spectral centre of gravity of the coronal clicks: about 4000 Hz for female and about 3000 Hz for male speakers of Mangetti Dune !Xung (three of four speakers), reflecting the small lingual cavity (`literature`) [miller_shah2009]
- **ǃ** ((post)alveolar click (chart label: (Post)alveolar); apical front closure behind the teeth, released abruptly; rear closure set by the accompaniment):
  - abrupt (sharp) release: a short, loud, pop-like transient with a fast rise to peak amplitude (`literature`) [miller_shah2009, wiki_click]
  - lowest spectral centre of gravity of the coronal clicks, around 1000 Hz for all four Mangetti Dune !Xung speakers, reflecting the large lingual cavity (`literature`) [miller_shah2009]
  - among the most intense click bursts; click peaks are often more than twice the peak-to-peak amplitude of the following vowel onset (about 6 dB), per Ladefoged & Traill as cited by Miller & Shah (`literature`) [miller_shah2009]
- **ǂ** (palatoalveolar click (chart label: Palatoalveolar; often called the palatal click); laminal front closure from the alveolar ridge to the hard palate, released fairly abruptly; rear closure set by the accompaniment):
  - abrupt (sharp) release class, but burst duration varies between speakers: for some it is about half that of the alveolar click, for others similar, which the authors attribute to differences in release dynamics (`literature`) [miller_shah2009, wiki_click]
  - relatively high spectral centre of gravity: about 2500-3000 Hz for female and 1500-2000 Hz for male Mangetti Dune !Xung speakers, second to the dental click (`literature`) [miller_shah2009]
- **ǁ** (alveolar lateral click (chart label: Alveolar lateral); front closure at the alveolar ridge released at the side(s) of the tongue; rear closure set by the accompaniment):
  - noisy (lateral, fricated) release: longer burst and longer rise time than the alveolar and palatal clicks, and lower amplitude (`literature`) [miller_shah2009, wiki_click]
  - intermediate spectral centre of gravity, around 2000 Hz in Mangetti Dune !Xung (`literature`) [miller_shah2009]
- **ɓ** (voiced bilabial implosive (chart label: Bilabial)):
  - voicing throughout the closure, typically strong: the larynx is lowered, timed toward the release, which enlarges the cavity behind the closure and keeps vocal-fold vibration going (`literature`) [goldstein_usc, wiki_implosive]
  - weak or no release burst: often no air moves through the opening at release (Hausa); in Sindhi intraoral pressure fell to about -2 to -5 cm H2O and air flowed inward at release (`literature`) [nihalani1991, wiki_implosive]
  - voicing may be aperiodic or creaky (reported for Hausa by Lindau, cited by Nihalani); Sindhi implosives had shorter closures than the plain voiced stop (`literature`) [nihalani1991]
  - voicing amplitude tends to rise during the closure (where a plain voiced stop's voicing fades), and the following vowel transitions point to a low F2 locus like [b] (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ɗ** (voiced dental/alveolar implosive (chart label: Dental/alveolar)):
  - voicing throughout the closure, maintained by lowering the larynx; the release has little or no burst and often no airflow (`literature`) [wiki_implosive, goldstein_usc]
  - voicing during closure can be creaky or aperiodic and varies between speakers and languages (`literature`) [nihalani1991]
  - voicing amplitude tends to rise during the closure; vowel transitions point to an alveolar F2 locus (about mid-range) like [d] (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ʄ** (voiced palatal implosive (chart label: Palatal)):
  - voicing throughout the closure with larynx lowering; weak or absent release burst (`literature`) [wiki_implosive, goldstein_usc]
  - implosives at places further back are harder to keep voiced because the cavity behind the closure is smaller (the airflow for voicing fills the partial vacuum sooner) (`literature`) [wiki_implosive]
  - vowel transitions with a high F2 locus like [ɟ]; possibly some frication at release as with palatal stops (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ɠ** (voiced velar implosive (chart label: Velar)):
  - voicing through the closure with larynx lowering; weak or absent release burst (`literature`) [wiki_implosive, goldstein_usc]
  - the small cavity behind a velar closure makes the implosive voicing hard to sustain, so velar implosives are rarer than bilabial and alveolar ones (`literature`) [wiki_implosive]
  - vowel transitions with the converging F2/F3 'velar pinch' of [ɡ] (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ʛ** (voiced uvular implosive (chart label: Uvular)):
  - voicing through the closure with larynx lowering; weak or absent release burst; the very small cavity behind a uvular closure makes it the hardest implosive to sustain (`literature`) [wiki_implosive, goldstein_usc]
  - vowel transitions like [ɢ]: low F2 and raised F1 near the consonant (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ʼ** (ejective: the consonant it follows is made with a glottalic egressive airstream; the glottis is closed and the larynx raised during the oral closure, compressing the air in the mouth and pharynx, which escapes at the oral release):
  - on stops: a release burst followed by a gap (the glottal closure is still held) before voicing starts, so voice onset is delayed relative to a plain unaspirated stop but with no aspiration noise; ejectives in Tsova-Tush had shorter VOT than the aspirated stops they contrast with (`literature`) [kye2021, hauk_hakim2019]
  - two typological patterns (Lindau 1984; Kingston 1985, 2005, as summarised by Kye): 'stiff' ejectives with long VOT, intense burst, raised f0 and modal or tense voice at the vowel onset, and fast amplitude rise; 'slack' ejectives with short VOT, ordinary burst, lowered f0, creaky vowel onset and slow amplitude rise; languages and speakers mix these properties (`literature`) [kye2021]
  - creakier voice quality (lower spectral tilt, H1*-H2*) at the onset of the following vowel and an f0 difference compared with aspirated stops, with marked differences between speakers (`literature`) [hauk_hakim2019]
  - often described as 'spat' or popping consonants, but may be weak and easy to mistake for plain stops; on fricatives (the chart's s’) the oral pressure leaks out during the frication, so the ejective quality is less salient (`literature`) [wiki_ejective]

### Expected acoustic correlates: other symbols

`literature` = found in a source the helper opened (named in brackets; `IPA_REFERENCES.md`). `recalled-unverified` = stated from memory with no opened source: to be verified before any value is used (it counts as `estimated`, playbook 1.5).

- **ʍ** (voiceless labial-velar fricative):
  - No voicing: the segment is a stretch of aperiodic noise with no harmonics and no voice bar; whatever formant structure shows comes from noise passing through a [w]-shaped tract. (`recalled-unverified`)
  - The tract shape is that of [w]: a labial and a velar constriction, like a close back rounded vowel; rounding at the lips lowers all formants, so the noise energy is concentrated low in the spectrum, with low F1 and low F2 shapes visible in the following vowel's onset transitions. (`literature`) [unc_ling520_approximants, mit_ocw_24915_vowels]
  - In English 'wh' words the sound is often realised as a voiceless approximant [w̥], a labialised glottal fricative [hʷ] or an [hw] sequence rather than strong velar friction, so the noise is typically weak and breathy rather than sibilant-like. (`literature`) [wiki_voiceless_labial_velar_fricative]
- **w** (voiced labial-velar approximant):
  - A glide: on a spectrogram it looks like a very short vowel next to a full vowel, with voicing and clear formants but shorter duration and no syllable nucleus. (`literature`) [unc_ling520_approximants]
  - Labial and velar constrictions give the formant pattern of a close back rounded vowel [u]: low F1 and low F2; lip rounding and protrusion lower all formants, F2 most for a back articulation. Transitions into the following vowel rise from these low values. (`literature`) [unc_ling520_approximants, mit_ocw_24915_vowels]
  - Slightly lower overall amplitude than the neighbouring vowel because of the narrow lip opening. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ɥ** (voiced labial-palatal approximant):
  - The non-syllabic counterpart of the close front rounded vowel [y] (French nuire, Mandarin yuè): a short glide with the formant pattern of [y]. (`literature`) [wiki_voiced_labial_palatal_approximant, unc_ling520_approximants]
  - Like [j] it has a low F1 and a high F2 from the palatal constriction, but lip rounding lowers the front-cavity resonance, which for front articulations is usually F3; so F3 (and to a lesser degree F2) sits lower than in [j], bringing F2 and F3 closer together. (`literature`) [mit_ocw_24915_vowels, unc_ling520_approximants]
  - Compressed (exolabial) and protruded (endolabial) rounding variants exist; the protruded type is described as acoustically between the compressed type and unrounded [j]. (`literature`) [wiki_voiced_labial_palatal_approximant]
- **ʜ** (voiceless epiglottal fricative):
  - Turbulent noise generated deep in the throat by the aryepiglottic folds; fibreoptic filming shows trilling of the aryepiglottic folds often accompanying the friction, which adds a low-frequency, irregular pulsing to the noise. (`literature`) [esling1996, wiki_epiglottal_consonant]
  - Auditory quality changes markedly with larynx height and with pitch register (raised versus lowered larynx). (`literature`) [esling1996]
  - A low pharyngeal constriction typically raises F1 and lowers F2 in adjacent vowels (pharyngealised colouring). (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ʢ** (voiced epiglottal fricative (IPA symbol list: voiced epiglottal fricative/approximant)):
  - Voicing combined with constriction (and often trilling) of the aryepiglottic folds; Esling lists a voiced fricative with aryepiglottic trilling, and notes that the commonest value of /ʕ/ is a voiced pharyngeal ('epiglottal') approximant, so the friction may be slight. (`literature`) [esling1996]
  - Because the constriction is so close to the vocal folds, voicing and friction interfere with each other, making a clearly fricated voiced epiglottal hard to produce and to distinguish. (`literature`) [wiki_epiglottal_consonant]
  - Expect a harsh, creaky-sounding voice quality with raised F1 and lowered F2 in the neighbouring vowels. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ʡ** (epiglottal plosive (voicing not specified on the chart)):
  - A complete closure made by the aryepiglottic folds against the epiglottis: a silent gap followed by an abrupt release; Esling describes it as a 'massive glottal stop'. (`literature`) [esling1996, wiki_epiglottal_consonant]
  - A truly voiced epiglottal stop may be impossible; intervocalically a voiced version surfaces as a tap. (`literature`) [wiki_epiglottal_consonant]
  - No oral burst spectrum to speak of; the place cue lies in pharyngealised transitions (high F1, low F2) and often creaky phonation at the edges of the closure. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ɕ** (voiceless alveolo-palatal fricative):
  - Sibilant noise whose centre of gravity lies between that of [s] (highest) and the Polish retroflex [ʂ] (lowest); for one female Polish speaker, read from the published figures, [ɕ] clustered at roughly 4 to 5.5 kHz against roughly 7 to 9 kHz for [s] and roughly 2.3 to 3.7 kHz for [ʂ], with no overlap between the three. (`literature`) [zygis_hamann2003]
  - It is a distinct category from a palatalised postalveolar [ʃʲ]: listeners separate the two reliably, and for the female speaker the [ʃʲ] centre of gravity fell between [ɕ] and [ʂ]; for the male speaker centre of gravity alone overlapped, so it is not a sufficient cue across speakers. (`literature`) [zygis_hamann2003]
  - The raised tongue body gives high F2 transitions into and out of neighbouring vowels, an [i]-like colouring. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ʑ** (voiced alveolo-palatal fricative):
  - Voiced counterpart of [ɕ]: periodic voicing (voice bar, low-frequency energy) combined with sibilant noise in the same mid-high region as [ɕ]; the published measurements covered only the voiceless member, so the noise placement is carried over by analogy. (`recalled-unverified`)
  - The Polish study that measured [ɕ] states that it restricted its perception tests and acoustic measurements to the voiceless segments, so no figures exist there for [ʑ]. (`literature`) [zygis_hamann2003]
  - Frication is weaker than in the voiceless [ɕ] because voicing reduces airflow through the constriction; high F2 transitions as for [ɕ]. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ɺ** (voiced alveolar lateral flap):
  - A single quick contact of the tongue against the alveolar ridge, with airflow over the sides of the tongue instead of the centre: acoustically a very brief lateral interval rather than a sustained [l]. (`literature`) [wiki_voiced_alveolar_lateral_flap]
  - During the lateral contact the spectrum should resemble a lateral: low F1, a relatively high F3, and antiformants from the pocket of air above the tongue acting as a side branch. (`literature`) [unc_ling520_approximants]
  - Duration is of the order of a tap (a few tens of milliseconds), much shorter than [l]; many languages described with a lateral flap actually have a flap indeterminate between central and lateral, so [ɾ]-like tokens are expected. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ɧ** (simultaneous ʃ and x (IPA symbol list: voiceless postalveolar-velar fricative)):
  - The chart defines the symbol as simultaneous [ʃ] and [x], but real realisations of the Swedish sj-sound vary widely (labiodental with velarisation and lip protrusion, or a velar fricative with weak friction), usually with distinct lip rounding; Ladefoged and Maddieson doubt that turbulence can be produced at two points at once in ordinary speech. (`literature`) [wiki_sj_sound]
  - Expected sound: a diffuse, relatively low-pitched, breathy frication without the strong high-frequency sibilant peak of [ʃ], darkened by lip rounding; centre of gravity lower than [ʃ]. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌͜** (tie bar (below): joins two symbols written as one segment, an affricate or a double articulation):
  - No sound of its own. For an affricate such as t͜s it marks a single segment: a stop closure released directly into a homorganic fricative, with the fricative portion shorter than an independent [s] and no intervening release burst plus vowel; for a double articulation it marks two simultaneous closures. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌͡** (tie bar (above): joins two symbols written as one segment, an affricate or a double articulation):
  - No sound of its own. For a labial-velar stop such as k͡p the two closures overlap so the release is one event: a single silent closure, a release with both velar and labial influence, and formant transitions in the following vowel that are dominated by the labial (falling-F2 type) pattern; for affricates as described under the bottom tie bar. (`recalled-unverified`)

### Expected acoustic correlates: vowels

`literature` = found in a source the helper opened (named in brackets; `IPA_REFERENCES.md`). `recalled-unverified` = stated from memory with no opened source: to be verified before any value is used (it counts as `estimated`, playbook 1.5).

- **i** (close front unrounded vowel):
  - Lowest F1 class on the chart (close). Highest F2 of all vowels (front, unrounded), with F3 also high and close to F2. Reference point for the top-left corner of the F1/F2 plane. (`literature`) [wiki_formant, wiki_vowel, umanitoba_formants]
  - Catford's average values for a male voice: F1 240 Hz, F2 2400 Hz (F2-F1 = 2160 Hz). Phonetician's cardinal-style reference values, single adult male voice type; no F3 given in this table. (`literature`) [wiki_formant] (as quoted there from catford2001, not opened)
  - American English men's average for the vowel listed as /i/ 'beet': F1 270 Hz, F2 2290 Hz, F3 3010 Hz. This is a language-specific vowel near [i], not a cardinal [i]; use as an order-of-magnitude check, not a target. (`literature`) [pb_princeton] (as quoted there from peterson_barney1952, not opened)
- **y** (close front rounded vowel):
  - F1 about as low as [i] (close). F2 and F3 both lower than for [i]: front rounding lowers F2 and F3, which is the main acoustic cue separating [y] from [i]; F2 still well above any central or back vowel. (`literature`) [wiki_formant, wiki_vowel, umanitoba_formants]
  - Catford's average values for a male voice: F1 235 Hz, F2 2100 Hz (F2-F1 = 1865 Hz). Phonetician's cardinal-style reference values, single adult male voice type; no F3 given in this table. (`literature`) [wiki_formant] (as quoted there from catford2001, not opened)
- **ɨ** (close central unrounded vowel):
  - F1 low, like [i] and [u] (close). F2 intermediate: below [i]/[y] and above [ɯ]/[u] (central). Unrounded, so F2 should sit above that of its rounded partner [ʉ]. (`literature`) [wiki_formant, wiki_vowel, umanitoba_formants]
  - No fetched source gave hertz values for this vowel; only the relative expectations above are supported. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ʉ** (close central rounded vowel):
  - F1 low (close). F2 central, lower than [ɨ] because of rounding but higher than [u]; F3 also lowered relative to [ɨ]. (`literature`) [wiki_formant, wiki_vowel, umanitoba_formants]
  - No fetched source gave hertz values for this vowel; only the relative expectations above are supported. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ɯ** (close back unrounded vowel):
  - F1 low (close). F2 low (back) but clearly higher than [u], because without lip rounding the back cavity is not lengthened; F2 tends toward the central region. (`literature`) [wiki_formant, wiki_vowel, umanitoba_formants]
  - Catford's average values for a male voice: F1 300 Hz, F2 1390 Hz (F2-F1 = 1090 Hz). Phonetician's cardinal-style reference values, single adult male voice type; no F3 given in this table. (`literature`) [wiki_formant] (as quoted there from catford2001, not opened)
- **u** (close back rounded vowel):
  - F1 low (close; rounding in back vowels lowers F1 further). F2 among the lowest on the chart (back + rounded), so F1 and F2 are close together; F3 relatively low. (`literature`) [wiki_formant, wiki_vowel, umanitoba_formants]
  - Catford's average values for a male voice: F1 250 Hz, F2 595 Hz (F2-F1 = 345 Hz). Phonetician's cardinal-style reference values, single adult male voice type; no F3 given in this table. (`literature`) [wiki_formant] (as quoted there from catford2001, not opened)
  - American English men's average for the vowel listed as /u/ 'boot': F1 300 Hz, F2 870 Hz, F3 2240 Hz. This is a language-specific vowel near [u], not a cardinal [u]; use as an order-of-magnitude check, not a target. (`literature`) [pb_princeton] (as quoted there from peterson_barney1952, not opened)
- **ɪ** (near-close near-front unrounded vowel):
  - F1 somewhat higher than [i] (slightly lower tongue). F2 somewhat lower than [i] (slightly retracted from front). Lies between [i] and [e] on the F1 axis. (`literature`) [wiki_formant, wiki_vowel, umanitoba_formants]

This is a language-specific vowel near [ɪ], not a cardinal [ɪ]; use as an order-of-magnitude check, not a target. (`literature`) [pb_princeton] (as quoted there from peterson_barney1952, not opened)
- **ʏ** (near-close near-front rounded vowel):
  - F1 somewhat higher than [y]; F2 somewhat lower than [y]. Compared with [ɪ], F2 and F3 are lower because of rounding. (`literature`) [wiki_formant, wiki_vowel, umanitoba_formants]
  - No fetched source gave hertz values for this vowel; only the relative expectations above are supported. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ʊ** (near-close near-back rounded vowel):
  - F1 somewhat higher than [u]; F2 somewhat higher than [u] (less back). Rounded, so F2 below that of an unrounded vowel at the same position. (`literature`) [wiki_formant, wiki_vowel, umanitoba_formants]

This is a language-specific vowel near [ʊ], not a cardinal [ʊ]; use as an order-of-magnitude check, not a target. (`literature`) [pb_princeton] (as quoted there from peterson_barney1952, not opened)
- **e** (close-mid front unrounded vowel):

- **ø** (close-mid front rounded vowel):

  - Catford's average values for a male voice: F1 370 Hz, F2 1900 Hz (F2-F1 = 1530 Hz). Phonetician's cardinal-style reference values, single adult male voice type; no F3 given in this table. (`literature`) [wiki_formant] (as quoted there from catford2001, not opened)
- **ɘ** (close-mid central unrounded vowel):
  - F1 close-mid, between [ɨ] and [ə]. F2 central: below [e]/[ø], above [ɤ]/[o]; above its rounded partner [ɵ]. (`literature`) [wiki_formant, wiki_vowel, umanitoba_formants]
  - No fetched source gave hertz values for this vowel; only the relative expectations above are supported. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ɵ** (close-mid central rounded vowel):
  - F1 close-mid. F2 central and lower than [ɘ] (rounding); F3 lowered relative to [ɘ]. (`literature`) [wiki_formant, wiki_vowel, umanitoba_formants]
  - No fetched source gave hertz values for this vowel; only the relative expectations above are supported. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ɤ** (close-mid back unrounded vowel):
  - F1 close-mid, like [o]. F2 low-ish (back) but clearly higher than [o] because unrounded; F2 drifts toward the central region. (`literature`) [wiki_formant, wiki_vowel, umanitoba_formants]
  - Catford's average values for a male voice: F1 460 Hz, F2 1310 Hz (F2-F1 = 850 Hz). Phonetician's cardinal-style reference values, single adult male voice type; no F3 given in this table. (`literature`) [wiki_formant] (as quoted there from catford2001, not opened)
- **o** (close-mid back rounded vowel):

### Expected acoustic correlates: diacritics

`literature` = found in a source the helper opened (named in brackets; `IPA_REFERENCES.md`). `recalled-unverified` = stated from memory with no opened source: to be verified before any value is used (it counts as `estimated`, playbook 1.5).

- **◌̥** (voiceless: the sound is produced without vocal-fold vibration (devoicing of a normally voiced segment)):
  - Voicing means vocal-fold vibration (periodicity); a voiceless segment therefore has no periodic glottal excitation and no F0 for its duration; on a stop this shows as no voicing during the closure (no voice bar) and voicing of a following vowel starting only at or after release. (`literature`) [wp_vot]
  - On a sonorant (e.g. n̥, l̥) the resonances are excited only by turbulent airflow, so the segment looks like weak aspiration-like noise with the base sound's formant/nasal resonances visible in the noise rather than in harmonics; overall amplitude is much lower than the voiced counterpart. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌̊** (voiceless (alternative placement above a symbol with a descender)):
  - Same as the under-ring: no periodic voicing and no F0 over the segment; for the nasal example ŋ̊ the velar nasal resonance is excited only by airflow noise. (`literature`) [wp_vot]
  - Voiceless nasals are weak, noisy and low in amplitude compared with voiced nasals. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌̬** (voiced: vocal-fold vibration during a segment that is normally voiceless):
  - Periodic voicing (F0, low-frequency voice bar) continues through the segment; on a stop this means voicing during the closure, i.e. a voice onset time at or below zero rather than a positive lag. (`literature`) [wp_vot]
  - On a fricative such as s̬ the high-frequency frication is accompanied by low-frequency periodic energy and the frication is usually weaker than in the voiceless form. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ʰ** (aspirated: a period of voicelessness (open glottis) after the release before voicing of the following sound begins):
  - Positive voice onset time: a gap of aspiration noise between the stop burst and the onset of voicing. VOT is the practical measure of aspiration; for velars, unaspirated about 20–30 ms, weakly aspirated about 50–60 ms, moderately aspirated about 80–90 ms, and much over 100 ms counts as strong; a strongly aspirating language (Navajo) has about twice the English duration (about 160 ms reported). (`literature`) [wp_vot]
  - During the aspiration the following vowel's formants (especially F2, F3) appear in turbulent noise before periodic voicing starts; F1 is weak in that interval. (`recalled-unverified`)
  - On a voiced base (the chart's dʰ) the result is acoustically similar to breathy voice: delay of full (modal) voicing after release. (`literature`) [wp_breathy]
- **◌̹** (more rounded: greater lip rounding than the base symbol implies):
  - Lip rounding lowers formants: F1 and F2 in back vowels, F2 and F3 in front vowels. More rounding therefore means lower F2 (and F3 for front vowels) than the base vowel. (`literature`) [wp_formant]
- **◌̜** (less rounded: less lip rounding than the base symbol implies):
  - The inverse of the rounding effect: less rounding leaves the formants that rounding lowers (F2, and F3 for front vowels; F1/F2 for back vowels) higher than for the fully rounded base vowel. (`literature`) [wp_formant]
- **◌̟** (advanced: articulated further forward than the base symbol implies):
  - Front vowels have higher F2; advancing a vowel (u̟) raises F2 relative to the base vowel. (`literature`) [wp_formant]
  - On consonants, a more forward place shifts the burst/frication spectrum upward and moves the F2 locus of the transitions toward that of the more forward place. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌̠** (retracted: articulated further back than the base symbol implies):
  - F2 relates to front–back position (front vowels have higher F2); retracting a vowel (e̠) lowers F2 relative to the base vowel. (`literature`) [wp_formant]
  - On consonants, a further-back place lowers the spectral peak of bursts and frication. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌̈** (centralized: tongue position moved toward the centre of the vowel space on the front–back dimension):
  - F2 carries front–back position, so centralization moves F2 toward the central (schwa-like) value: lower F2 for a front base vowel, higher F2 for a back base vowel. (`literature`) [wp_formant]
- **◌̽** (mid-centralized: moved toward the mid-central (schwa) position, in height as well as backness):
  - Both formants move toward the mid-central region: F2 toward the central value (as for centralized) and F1 toward a mid value (F1 is higher for open vowels, lower for close vowels). (`literature`) [wp_formant]
- **◌̩** (syllabic: the consonant forms the nucleus of a syllable):
  - A syllabic consonant is a syllable nucleus by itself (as m, n, l in rhythm, sudden, awful). (`literature`) [wp_syllabic]
  - As a nucleus it has longer duration and greater relative intensity than the same consonant in onset or coda, and carries the syllable's pitch and stress; the steady sonorant resonances (nasal murmur, lateral or rhotic formants) fill the time a vowel would. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌̯** (non-syllabic: the vowel is not the syllable nucleus (e.g. the off-glide of a diphthong)):
  - A non-syllabic vowel is short and lower in intensity than the nucleus it attaches to, with formants mainly in transition toward or away from the nucleus rather than a steady state. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **˞** (rhoticity: r-colouring of a vowel (tongue tip raised/retroflexed or tongue bunched, often with pharyngeal constriction)):
  - The defining acoustic property of an r-coloured vowel is a lowering of F3. (`literature`) [wp_rcolored, wp_formant]
  - For comparison, the English r consonant has a very low F3, well below 2000 Hz. (`literature`) [wp_formant]
- **◌̤** (breathy voiced: vocal folds vibrate with lower tension and incomplete closure, letting more air escape (murmur)):
  - Higher H1*-H2* (stronger first harmonic relative to the second, i.e. steeper spectral tilt) than modal voice; this was the one measure that separated phonation types in all four languages studied, with breathy/lax voice showing the highest values. (`literature`) [keating2011]
  - Lower electroglottographic contact quotient (less vocal-fold contact) and more noise (lower cepstral peak prominence / harmonics-to-noise) than modal voice. (`literature`) [keating2011]
  - In a breathy stop such as b̤ there is a delay in the onset of full modal voicing after release, similar to aspiration. (`literature`) [wp_breathy]
  - Breathy consonants in some languages lower the pitch (F0) of a following vowel. (`literature`) [wp_breathy]
- **◌̰** (creaky voiced: constricted glottis with slow, irregular vocal-fold vibration (laryngealization)):
  - Prototypical creak has a low F0, an irregular F0 (shows up as jitter and lower harmonics-to-noise ratio / cepstral peak prominence) and a constricted glottis. (`literature`) [keating2015]
  - Lower H1 and lower H1-H2 (and other harmonic differences), i.e. less spectral tilt, than modal voice. (`literature`) [keating2015]
  - Sub-types differ: vocal fry has damped pulses, regular F0 and narrow formant bandwidths; multiply-pulsed creak shows subharmonics (high subharmonic-to-harmonic ratio); non-constricted creak can show higher, not lower, H1-H2. No single measure identifies all creak. (`literature`) [keating2015]
- **◌̼** (linguolabial: tongue tip or blade against the upper lip):
  - Formant transitions resemble those of labial consonants (low F2 locus) because the constriction is at the lips, while the burst/frication is produced by the tongue against the lip; the precise spectral differences from bilabials are not established here. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ʷ** (labialized: secondary lip rounding (often with raised tongue back) added to the consonant):
  - Lip rounding lowers formants (F2, and F3 in front contexts), so the release and transitions into the next vowel show lowered F2/F3 compared with the plain consonant. (`literature`) [wp_formant]
  - Burst and frication spectra are lowered in frequency by the lip rounding. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ʲ** (palatalized: secondary raising of the tongue body toward the hard palate):
  - i-like transitions: raised F2 (and often F3) at the release and into the following vowel, and higher-frequency burst/frication spectra than the plain consonant. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ˠ** (velarized: secondary raising of the back of the tongue toward the velum):
  - Lowered F2 at the consonant and in adjacent vowel transitions compared with the plain consonant (as in English 'dark' l). (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ˤ** (pharyngealized: secondary constriction of the pharynx (tongue root/epiglottis retracted)):
  - Vowels next to pharyngealized consonants are more open (higher F1) and more back (lower F2), with F1 and F2 closer together; the study also found tense/pressed voice quality in those vowels. (`literature`) [altamimi2017]
  - When lip protrusion accompanies the pharyngeal constriction, F3 is expected to fall in front vowels; in back vowels F3 can rise. (`literature`) [altamimi2017]
- **◌̴** (velarized or pharyngealized: a cover mark for velarization, uvularization or pharyngealization, written through the letter):
  - Pharyngealized realizations: higher F1 and lower F2 in adjacent vowels. (`literature`) [altamimi2017]
  - Velarized realizations (e.g. ɫ, dark l): lowered F2 compared with the plain consonant. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌̝** (raised: articulated with a closer constriction (higher tongue) than the base symbol implies):
  - On a vowel, raising lowers F1 (open vowels have higher F1, close vowels lower F1). (`literature`) [wp_formant]
  - On an approximant, raising narrows the constriction enough to make turbulent frication: the chart gives ɹ̝ as a voiced alveolar fricative (non-sibilant), so frication noise is added to the base sound. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌̞** (lowered: articulated with a more open constriction (lower tongue) than the base symbol implies):
  - On a vowel, lowering raises F1. (`literature`) [wp_formant]
  - On a fricative, lowering widens the constriction so frication noise disappears and the sound becomes an approximant with clear formants: the chart gives β̞ as a voiced bilabial approximant. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌̘** (advanced tongue root (ATR): tongue root moved forward and larynx lowered, expanding the pharynx):
  - ATR vowels are often heard as 'brighter', associated with narrower formant bandwidths; larynx lowering can add a breathy quality. (`literature`) [wp_atr]
  - Lower F1 than the corresponding RTR (or plain) vowel, because the expanded pharynx lengthens and widens the back cavity. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌̙** (retracted tongue root (RTR): tongue root pulled back, narrowing the pharynx):
  - Higher F1 than the ATR counterpart, often with a lower F2; voice quality may be less breathy / more tense than ATR vowels. (`recalled-unverified`)
  - Heard as less 'bright' than ATR vowels (ATR vowels have narrower formants). (`literature`) [wp_atr]
- **◌̪** (dental: tongue tip or blade against the upper teeth):
  - Compared with alveolar stops, dental stops tend to have a weaker, more diffuse (flatter) burst spectrum and slightly lower F2 locus in transitions; dental fricatives have weak, diffuse noise. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌̺** (apical: articulated with the tongue tip):
  - Compared with laminal articulations, apical stops and fricatives tend to have lower-frequency, more compact burst/frication spectra and a sharper release; apicals can show some F3/F4 lowering in transitions. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌̻** (laminal: articulated with the tongue blade):
  - Compared with apical articulations, laminal stops tend to have more diffuse, higher-frequency burst energy and longer, noisier releases (more affrication); laminal sibilants have a higher spectral centre of gravity. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌̃** (nasalized: the velum is lowered so air resonates in the nasal cavity as well as the mouth):
  - A1 (amplitude of the harmonic at F1) drops and A1-P0 (F1 amplitude minus the low-frequency nasal peak, measured around 250 Hz) decreases; the lowering of A1 is the main driver. P0 gains prominence relative to the neighbouring harmonics even when its absolute amplitude does not rise. A1-P0 was the most reliable measure across vowels, speakers and languages (English and French). (`literature`) [styler2014]
  - Formant-frequency changes with nasality are language-specific rather than a direct consequence of nasal coupling. (`literature`) [styler2014]
  - Nasal coupling also adds pole-zero pairs and widens the F1 bandwidth, flattening the low-frequency spectrum. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ⁿ** (nasal release: the oral closure of a stop is released by lowering the velum, so air escapes through the nose (e.g. English sudden)):
  - No oral release burst; the stop closure goes straight into a nasal: the velum lowers while the mouth stays closed, giving an audible nasal release. (`literature`) [wp_nasalrelease]
  - Spectrally the stop closure is followed directly by a nasal murmur (strong low-frequency resonance, weak higher formants, antiresonances) instead of a burst and vowel transitions. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ˡ** (lateral release: the stop is released by lowering the sides of the tongue into a lateral (e.g. English middle)):
  - The stop is released into a lateral consonant rather than with a central burst; in English it is often transcribed as having no audible release. (`literature`) [wp_lateralrelease]
  - The lateral that follows has its typical resonances; [l] usually has an extra formant around 1500 Hz. (`literature`) [wp_formant]
  - The burst is weak or absent and lateral formant structure begins immediately at release. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **◌̚** (no audible release: the stop closure is held without an audible release burst (unreleased / checked stop)):
  - No release burst: nothing marks the end of the oral closure; in clusters the release happens during the next consonant's closure and is masked. Any aspiration contrast is neutralized. (`literature`) [wp_noaudible]
  - The stop is cued only by the formant transitions of the preceding vowel and by the silent closure. (`recalled-unverified`)

### Expected acoustic correlates: suprasegmentals

`literature` = found in a source the helper opened (named in brackets; `IPA_REFERENCES.md`). `recalled-unverified` = stated from memory with no opened source: to be verified before any value is used (it counts as `estimated`, playbook 1.5).

- **ˈ** (Marks the syllable that carries the word's main stress. The mark is written before the stressed syllable, not before its vowel.):
  - Longer syllable. In American English two-syllable words with no pitch accent, stressed syllables were about 1.2 to 1.3 times as long as the same syllables unstressed (reiterant speech 189 vs 159 ms and 198 vs 157 ms; real words 184 vs 147 ms and 272 vs 236 ms). With accent removed, duration was the best single cue to stress position. (`literature`) [sluijter1996]
  - Native English speakers' stressed vowels were on average 1.26 times as long as the unstressed ones in disyllabic words read in isolation. (`literature`) [guo2022]
  - Flatter spectral tilt (more high-frequency energy, i.e. smaller H1-A2 and H1-A3) in stressed vowels: a sharper glottal closure. Unstressed vowels have more sinusoidal glottal pulses. This was the second-best cue after duration and about as strong. (`literature`) [sluijter1996]
  - Overall intensity rises only a little from stress alone (about 1 to 3 dB). A much larger rise comes when the syllable also carries a pitch accent. (`literature`) [sluijter1996]
  - F0: stress alone gives little or no F0 cue. The F0 excursion (rise-fall) belongs to the pitch accent, which in a focused word lands on the primary-stressed syllable. In citation-form English words the stressed syllable had about 13% higher F0 than the unstressed one (S/U ratio 1.13). (`literature`) [sluijter1996, guo2022]
  - Fuller, more peripheral vowel quality. Unstressed vowels move toward schwa (more so in English than in Dutch). Some languages, such as Finnish and Spanish, do not reduce unstressed vowels. (`literature`) [sluijter1996, wiki_stress]
  - In Finnish, stress adds roughly 100 ms to the vowel of the stressed syllable. (`literature`) [wiki_chroneme]
- **ˌ** (Marks a syllable that is stressed relative to unstressed syllables but less strongly than the primary-stressed one. Written before the syllable.):
  - Prominence between primary-stressed and unstressed syllables. (`literature`) [wiki_stress]
  - Expected: a full (unreduced) vowel and a duration between unstressed and primary-stressed, with the flatter spectral tilt of stressed vowels. Because the pitch accent normally goes to the primary stress, there is usually no large F0 excursion, so duration and vowel quality do most of the work. None of the fetched sources measured secondary stress separately. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **ː** (The preceding segment (vowel or consonant) is held longer. On a consonant it marks a long consonant (geminate).):
  - Vowels in quantity languages are roughly twice as long or more. Pooled Estonian figures: short stressed vowel 93 ms, long 175 ms, overlong 228 ms, giving long/short ratios of about 1.9 and 2.5. Japanese long vowels are 2.4 to 3.2 times as long as short ones in minimal pairs (normal-rate example: 82 vs 209 ms, ratio 2.55). One read corpus measured 79 vs 167 ms, a ratio of 2.1. (`literature`) [meister2015]
  - The long/short ratio stays about the same across speaking rates (Japanese 2.55 at slow, normal and fast rates; Estonian stable across studies despite large absolute variation). Length should therefore be applied as a duration ratio, not a fixed number of milliseconds. (`literature`) [meister2015]
  - Long consonants have longer closure or frication. Estonian geminate/singleton ratios are about 2.0 (long) and 2.4 (overlong): singletons 71 ms, geminates 140 and 173 ms. Japanese geminate stops are about 2.5 to 2.6 times as long as singletons (66.7 vs 176 ms; 93 vs 235 ms). (`literature`) [meister2015]
  - For reference, phonemically short first-syllable /a/ measured about 85 to 89 ms in Finnish and Japanese production data. (`literature`) [iseijaakkola2004]
  - Pitch can be a secondary cue to length: in Estonian, F0 shape helps tell the long and overlong degrees apart. (`literature`) [meister2015, wiki_chroneme]
  - Non-phonemic length differences are much smaller. The English tense/lax vowel duration ratio is about 1.3. (`literature`) [meister2015]
- **ˑ** (The preceding segment is somewhat longer than short but shorter than long.):
  - Duration between the plain short and the ː-long value of the same segment. No fetched source gives a ratio for ˑ as such. (`recalled-unverified`)
  - Finnish example: a phonemically short vowel in the second syllable after a light (single short vowel) first syllable is called half-long. It is clearly longer than the roughly 40 ms of an ordinary unstressed short vowel (unstressed long vowels are about 70 ms). (`literature`) [wiki_chroneme]
  - Related Estonian measurement: after a short stressed vowel (Q1 words), the unstressed second-syllable vowel is lengthened to about 111 ms, against 85 ms after a long (Q2) and 63 ms after an overlong (Q3) syllable. (Treating this lengthened vowel as half-long is our interpretation, not the source's.) (`literature`) [meister2015]
  - An English example in the literature writes ˑ on the stressed vowel of police [pə̆ˈliˑs]. (`literature`) [wiki_extrashort]
- **◌̆** (The segment under the mark (usually a vowel) is extra short while keeping its quality. On a consonant the breve can stand for a very brief articulation, for example a flap that has no letter of its own.):
  - Very brief segment, shorter than an ordinary short one of the same quality, as in the reduced schwa of police [pə̆ˈliˑs]. On a stop letter it shows a flap-like, very short closure. (`literature`) [wiki_extrashort]
  - For scale: Finnish unstressed short vowels are about 40 ms. English unstressed monophthongs are reported to be about half as long as stressed ones. (`literature`) [wiki_chroneme, guo2022]
  - Expected: weak amplitude, formants that may not reach their targets (centralised), sometimes partly devoiced; often used for epenthetic or transitional vowels. No fetched source gives millisecond values for breve-marked segments. (`recalled-unverified`)
- **|** (Boundary of a minor prosodic group (foot group). It is a phrasing mark, not an articulation.):
  - Lengthening before the boundary, usually without a silent pause. In English radio news, at a ToBI break 3 (intermediate phrase) the last syllable of a monosyllabic word was 297 ms, against 160 ms at an ordinary word boundary (break 1) and 223 ms at break 2 (about 1.9 times break 1). The following silence was almost nil (word plus silence 301 ms). In polysyllabic words the figures were 253 vs 195 ms. (`literature`) [wang2019]
  - In ToBI, break 3 is required wherever a phrase accent ends an intermediate phrase, so a pitch movement (phrase accent) at the end of the group is expected even when there is no pause. (`literature`) [wang2019]
  - Language-specific: in Mandarin, syllables before a minor phrase boundary were no longer than before higher boundaries (about 253 to 261 ms). The cross-boundary time (syllable plus silence) still increased with boundary strength. (`literature`) [wang2019]
  - Prosodic-unit boundaries in general are marked by pitch movement, pre-boundary lengthening and pauses, with pitch reset between declination units. (`literature`) [wiki_prosodic_unit]
- **‖** (Boundary of a major prosodic group (intonation phrase). Often coincides with a breath or pause.):
  - Strongest pre-boundary lengthening plus a silent pause. In English radio news, at a ToBI break 4 (full intonation phrase) monosyllabic words had a final syllable of 350 ms (about 2.2 times the 160 ms at an ordinary word boundary) followed by about 94 ms of silence on average (syllable plus silence 444 ms). Polysyllabic words: 299 ms plus about 104 ms of silence (403 ms). (`literature`) [wang2019]
  - Stronger boundaries get more pre-boundary lengthening and more often a silent pause. Lengthening and pausing can trade off against each other, and the total time across the boundary is the most consistent marker of boundary strength. (`literature`) [wang2019]
  - In German radio news, intonation-phrase-final syllables averaged 259 ms unaccented and 383 ms accented. Pauses after them were long enough that the study used 500 ms as the dividing line between weak and strong IP boundaries. (`literature`) [andreeva2020]
  - F0: a boundary tone at the end of the group, then pitch reset (a restart of declination) at the start of the next group. Breathing occurs only at higher-level boundaries. (`literature`) [wiki_prosodic_unit]
  - Voice quality: phrase-final creak is common, and after a pause of more than 50 ms a vowel-initial word was glottalised 94 to 100% of the time in American English radio speech. Glottal onsets are also more likely at the start of a full intonation phrase than of an intermediate phrase (shown for reduced vowels). (`literature`) [dilley1995]
- **.** (Marks where one syllable ends and the next begins. On the chart it separates two vowels in hiatus (re.act), which are therefore not a diphthong.):
  - No pause and no phrase-level lengthening. The break changes how segments are grouped. In hiatus (ɹi.ækt) each vowel has its own amplitude peak and steady state, with a transition between them instead of one gliding diphthong nucleus. A consonant after the break is realised as an onset (in English, for example, voiceless stops are aspirated at the start of a stressed syllable). (`recalled-unverified`)
  - A vowel that begins a new syllable or word may be marked by a glottal onset (glottal stop, irregular pitch periods, an F0 dip) or just by an amplitude dip that listeners hear as an onset. In American English this happens more often at phrase starts and on pitch-accented syllables. (`literature`) [dilley1995]
  - Explicit syllable dots are optional in transcription. Inside a word a stress mark also marks a syllable break (/ʌn.dər.ˈstʊd/ = /ʌndərˈstʊd/). (`literature`) [wiki_syllable]
- **‿** (Two words are joined with no break between them, for example a linking or liaison consonant that becomes the onset of the next word's syllable (French les amis /lɛ.z‿a.mi/), or two words forming one phonological unit (hot dog /ˈhɒt‿dɒɡ/).):
  - Resyllabification: the word-final consonant is produced as the onset of the next word's first syllable (liaison). (`literature`) [wiki_syllable]
  - No glottal stop or glottalisation before the vowel-initial second word. Without linking, British English speakers insert a glottal stop before vowel-initial words in up to 50% of cases in some contexts (11.8% after obstruents, 19.0% after sonorants, 29.0% after vowels). American English radio speakers glottalised such vowels only 2 to 15% of the time inside a phrase on unaccented words. (`literature`) [fuchs2015, dilley1995]
  - No pause and no pre-boundary lengthening at the junction. Voicing and formant transitions run continuously across the word boundary, as inside a word. (`recalled-unverified`)

- The stress sources disagree on F0 and intensity: Sluijter and van Heuven find that F0 and overall intensity cue pitch accent, not stress, while Guo reports a stressed/unstressed F0 ratio of 1.13 for citation-form English words, where stress and accent are confounded.
- Fuchs (2015) was read only as the Interspeech abstract page, not the full paper.
- Licences of the conference papers (ISCA/ICPhS) are not stated on the copies read. The JEFUL article licence was not checked.
- NFC normalisation turns the chart example e + U+0306 into the precomposed U+0115 (ĕ). The engine will meet both forms.
- Arguable membership: Global rise ↗ and Global fall ↘ have IPA numbers 510-511 in the same 500 prosody series but are printed in the Tones and word accents box, so they are left to the tones helper.

### Tones and word accents

- Rising-falling tone letter: the IPA's own symbol list gives 02E7 + 02E6 + 02E8 (˧˦˨, levels 3-4-2), and pixel measurement of the Doulos SIL and DejaVu Sans sub-chart images agrees (start 3, peak 4, end near 2), as does Wikipedia's IPA article ('since the 2018 revision'). The IPA Kiel-font image (the Association's recommended chart) draws a ligature whose end meets the stave nearer level 3 (˧˦˧). Recorded ˧˦˨; the Kiel glyph may be a font drawing difference.
- Wikipedia's 'IPA number' article lists 533 as ˨˦˨ (2-4-2) and Wikipedia's IPA article says the 1999 Handbook chart had ˦˥˦; both differ from the current IPA list (˧˦˨). The Handbook itself was not opened.
- The IPA symbol list's TIPA codes disagree with its own hex values for three contour letters: high rising \tone{45} vs 02E7+02E5 (35), low rising \tone{12} vs 02E9+02E7 (13), rising-falling \tone{454} vs 02E7+02E6+02E8 (342). The hex values, the chart images and Wikipedia agree, so the hex values were used.
- Contour tone letters have no code points of their own; they are sequences of U+02E5..U+02E9 that fonts ligate. The Doulos chart PDF's text layer maps them to U+FFFD, so the sequences come from the IPA symbol list and image measurement, not from extractable chart text.
- equivalent_to pairs each diacritic with the tone letter printed in the same chart row, as the brief asks. The Handbook (as quoted by Wikipedia, not opened) says this pairing is only a layout simplification: diacritics have at most doubled marks for extra high/low and coarse H/M/L contour points, letters have five levels. A synthesiser should not treat é as exactly ˦ or ě as exactly ˩˥.
- Downstep and upstep: the chart images show plain-looking arrows. The IPA symbol list and the Doulos text layer identify them as U+A71C and U+A71B (raised modifier arrows). U+2193/U+2191 and the raised exclamation marks U+A71D/U+A71E are common substitutes in the literature (listed under alt_codepoints) but are not what the chart encodes.
- Global rise/fall U+2197/U+2198 have emoji presentation sequences in Unicode 18.0; U+FE0E text style is listed as an alternative, not as the chart's code.
- Membership: the section heading is 'Tones and word accents', but no symbol is specific to word accents (pitch accent); all 24 printed symbols are counted. Global rise and fall are intonation marks and downstep and upstep are register shifts, not tones, but the chart prints all four in this section, so they are included here. The word 'or' printed between the first diacritic and letter in the full chart is not an entry.
- Hz and ms values come from Mandarin (Xu 1997: eight male Beijing speakers in isolation; Wang et al. 2022: downstep). No fetched source gives absolute F0 values for the IPA's five levels in general; tone levels are relative to the speaker's range. Chao's semitone spacing is taken from Wikipedia's report, not from Chao (1956, 1968) directly. Xu and Sun (2002)'s pitch-rise time formula is quoted from Wang et al. (2022); the original was not opened.
- No fetched source gave measurements for extra-high, extra-low, low-rising or rising-falling tones specifically, or for upstep magnitude; those correlates are in words and tagged recalled-unverified or rely on general sources.
